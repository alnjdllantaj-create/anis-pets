Pet Rabbit Care: The Right Diet, Safe Housing and 5 Deadly Mistakes

Rabbits are among the gentlest, quietest companions in the home — and also among the most unintentionally mis-cared-for animals. This guide gets the basics right from day one.

Food: the pyramid you must never flip

  • Unlimited hay:80–90% of the diet; grass hay (timothy) for adults. Constant chewing files down a rabbit's ever-growing teeth and protects the digestive tract.
  • Daily leafy greens:parsley, coriander, rocket, radish tops — a washed handful about the size of the rabbit's head.
  • Pellets in limited amounts:one to two tablespoons a day for an adult; overfeeding pellets is a common cause of obesity and of refusing hay.
  • Fruit is "candy":a thumb-sized piece twice a week at most; its sugar upsets the gut bacteria.
  • Never feed:onion, garlic, potato, pulses, chocolate, and any bread or pastry.

Housing: space, not a cage

A rabbit needs room to stretch out fully and to hop; a cage is a "bedroom", not a home. Provide a large indoor pen or a rabbit-proofed room (covered cables, toxic plants out of reach), with non-slip flooring to protect the joints.

Hygiene and everyday care

  • Clean the litter area daily; rabbits learn to use a litter box just like cats.
  • Never bathe a rabbit; stress and drowning are real risks — regular brushing is enough, especially during moulting.
  • Trim the nails every 4–6 weeks if they don't wear down naturally.

Five common mistakes that can kill

  1. Carrots and lettuce as staple food:a cartoon myth; carrots are high in sugar and iceberg lettuce is nutritionally near-worthless.
  2. Ignoring 12 hours without food:a rabbit's gut never stops safely; any refusal to eat with a silent gut is an immediate veterinary emergency (GI stasis).
  3. Isolation:rabbits are social animals; prolonged solitude means depression and lethargy.
  4. Heat:above 30 °C there is heatstroke risk; a cool, shaded spot in summer is non-negotiable.
  5. Lifting a rabbit by ears or scruff:always support hindquarters and chest together; the floor is better for him than long carried cuddles.

FAQ

Is a rabbit suitable for small children?

As a "watch-and-gently-interact" pet, yes; but the actual care is always an adult's responsibility — rabbit bones are fragile and improper handling endangers both rabbit and child.

Does a rabbit need vaccinations?

It depends on the country and its prevailing diseases; ask a local vet about a schedule appropriate for your area.

One rabbit or two?

A compatible bonded pair (with proper neutering) gives a far richer life; if you keep just one, your long daily presence is an essential substitute.
